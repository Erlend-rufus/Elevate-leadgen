AI Overviews reduce clicks, but mainly on informational searches — and the effect on a typical service business is smaller than the headlines suggest. In [Pew Research Center analysis](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), users clicked a traditional result in 8% of searches with an AI summary, against 15% without. The sensible response is to measure enquiries, not visits, and make your pages worth citing.

## What the best current evidence actually shows

Most of what you read about AI Overviews is opinion dressed as data. The strongest published evidence so far comes from the [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), which analysed the browsing of around 900 US adults — roughly 68,900 Google searches from March 2025. Its findings, in plain terms:

- When a search produced an AI summary, users clicked a traditional result in 8% of cases. Without a summary, they clicked in 15% of cases.
- Users clicked one of the sources cited inside the summary in only 1% of searches.
- Sessions ended without any click more often when a summary appeared: 26% versus 16%.
- Only 18% of all searches produced a summary at all. Long, question-style queries were far more likely to trigger one than short, transactional ones.

Two honest caveats. The panel is American, and the picture keeps moving as Google adjusts the feature. Treat these as the best available signal, not settled law.

For wider context: [Gartner predicted in February 2024](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-02-19-gartner-predicts-search-engine-volume-will-drop-25-percent-by-2026-due-to-ai-chatbots-and-other-virtual-agents) that traditional search volume would fall 25% by 2026. That is a forecast from a respected analysis house, not a measurement — worth knowing, dangerous to treat as fate.

## What "zero-click" actually means for your numbers

The phrase sounds like an ending. It is better read as a re-routing. A searcher who gets their answer from a summary and then leaves was, in most cases, never going to become a client on that visit — they wanted information, not a supplier. Some of them return later, often by searching your name directly or asking an assistant who to use. That later visit does not show up as a click from the original query, which is exactly why raw session counts have become a misleading scoreboard.

The numbers that still matter are the ones closest to money: branded searches, calls, form submissions, booked appointments. Watch those over six months rather than sessions over six weeks, and the picture is usually far calmer than the industry conversation suggests.

## Which of your searches are actually affected

This is the part the panic headlines skip. Pew's data shows AI summaries cluster on informational queries: "how long does conveyancing take", "what does a knee replacement cost privately", "do I need planning permission for a loft conversion". These are the searches where the answer, not the link, is the product.

The searches that pay your bills look different. "Emergency plumber Leeds", "employment solicitor near me", "book MOT Garforth" — short, local, transactional. These rarely produce a summary, and when they do, the person still has to phone someone. A leak cannot be fixed by a paragraph of generated text.

So the practical question is not "will AI Overviews kill my traffic?" but "which parts of my traffic were ever going to convert?" Losing visits from people who only wanted a free answer is not the same as losing enquiries. If you publish informational content — guides, explainers, cost breakdowns — treat it honestly from now on: its job is to build familiarity and earn citations, and the visitors who do click through arrive better informed than they used to.

## Google's own advice, in plain terms

Google's [AI features documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) makes three points worth hearing directly.

First, there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews: if your page is indexed and eligible for normal search, it is eligible for the AI features. Second, there is no special markup or AI-specific file to add. Third — and most useful — Google suggests judging these surfaces by conversions: sales, sign-ups, engaged readers, people who looked you up afterwards, rather than raw click counts.

Its broader [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) points the same way: content created to genuinely help a reader is what the systems are built to reward, on every surface.

## The practical response for a service business

Five moves, in order of leverage:

1. **Rebase your measurement.** Track phone calls, form submissions and booked jobs — not sessions. If visits fall but enquiries hold, nothing important has been lost.
2. **Win the citation.** On your informational pages, answer the question directly in the first paragraph, use clear headings, name an author, date the content. Being cited inside the summary is the new page one.
3. **Strengthen what AI cannot replace.** Google Business Profile, genuine reviews, local pages with real detail. These feed the map pack and the assistants at the same time.
4. **Keep your SEO fundamentals in order.** Fast, crawlable, well-structured pages feed both the old surface and the new one. Our [SEO service](/services/seo) is built on exactly that principle.
5. **Add the GEO layer deliberately.** Structuring content so assistants can extract, verify and cite it is a distinct discipline. That is what our [GEO service](/services/geo) does.

One more asset belongs on this list, because it is immune to all of the above: your existing relationships. An email list, a phone book of past clients, a reputation that generates word of mouth — no algorithm update takes those away. The businesses most frightened by zero-click search are usually the ones that never built a channel they own. That is worth fixing regardless of what Google does next.

## What not to do

Three reactions look tempting and are all mistakes.

Do not block AI systems in a panic. Opting out removes you from the citation pool entirely, and the summary usually appears anyway — quoting someone else.

Do not flood your site with machine-generated articles. Volume without substance is precisely what Google's spam systems target, and a new domain has no authority cushion to absorb the mistake.

And do not fire your SEO effort because of a headline. The searches that produce revenue are the least affected ones, and the fundamentals that win there are the same ones that get you cited.

Worth stating plainly: none of this requires you to become a publisher. A service business needs a handful of genuinely useful pages, not a content farm. Ten pages that answer real questions well will outperform a hundred thin ones, on every surface, old or new.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will AI Overviews destroy my website traffic?

The best current evidence says no for most service businesses. Summaries appear on a minority of searches, mostly informational ones, and transactional local queries are barely touched. Measure enquiries, and you will see the real picture.

### Can I opt out of AI Overviews?

Partially. Standard snippet controls can limit or remove your content from summaries, but opting out means you cannot be cited either. For most businesses, being cited is worth more than the clicks it sometimes replaces.

### Is SEO still worth doing?

Yes. Google's own documentation confirms the same eligibility rules feed AI features, and the searches that drive enquiries remain heavily click-driven. What changes is the emphasis: direct answers, clear structure and real expertise matter more than ever.

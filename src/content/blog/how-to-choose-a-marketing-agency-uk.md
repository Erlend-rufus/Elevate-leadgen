Choose your next agency the way you would check any significant supplier: verify the company, test the claims, read the contract and start small. Most of what went wrong last time was visible before you signed — in the promises, the paperwork and the questions that got vague answers. This time, look for evidence over enthusiasm.

## Why smart business owners get burned

It rarely starts with incompetence. It starts with a pitch built on the things owners want to hear: guaranteed rankings, dramatic growth curves, a flood of leads. Then the contract is signed, the senior people vanish, reports fill up with impressions and clicks, and twelve months later the honest answer to "what did we get?" is "we're not sure".

None of this is rare. The Advertising Standards Authority says [around 70% of the complaints it receives each year](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/misleading-advertising.html) are considered under the rules on misleading advertising. Exaggeration is not an aberration in this industry; it is close to a business model. The good news is that the same standards regulators apply to agencies' ads can be applied by you to agencies' pitches.

## Check the company before the pitch

This takes twenty minutes and almost nobody does it.

Look the agency up on [Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/) — the official register, free to search. Check three things:

- **How long the company has existed.** A two-year-old agency is not automatically a bad bet, but "a decade of experience" from a company incorporated last spring is a finding in itself.
- **The filing history.** Late accounts, a trail of dissolved predecessor companies, or directors who cycle in and out are all visible here.
- **Who actually owns and runs it.** The directors and persons with significant control are listed by name. Search those names. Previous ventures follow people around.

You are not looking for perfection. You are looking for the gap between the story on the website and the facts on the register.

One refinement that pays off: if you are down to two finalists, run this check on both and compare like with like. The agency that wins the pitch is not always the one that survives the paperwork. Half an hour of comparison here is cheaper than six months of discovering it the slow way.

## Test the claims like a regulator would

The UK advertising rules put the burden of proof on the marketer. [CAP Code rule 3.7](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/substantiation.html) requires documentary evidence, held in advance, for any claim a consumer would take as objective. You can adopt exactly the same standard in your first meeting.

When an agency says it delivers results, ask: for whom, measured how, over what period — and can I see it? The point is not the answer alone; it is the reaction. Agencies with real evidence produce it comfortably. Agencies with good pitch decks produce adjectives.

Apply the same test to testimonials. [CAP rules](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/testimonials-and-endorsements.html) require marketers to hold documentary evidence that testimonials are genuine, including contact details for the person who gave them. Your version: ask for two or three clients you can actually call. Logos on a website prove a designer was paid. A phone call proves the work.

And when you make those calls, ask the questions the case study skips: What was the first month actually like? What did the agency get wrong, and how did they handle it? Would you sign the same contract again? Past clients answer these more honestly than any sales process ever will.

## Read the contract for the three traps

The contract tells you how an agency expects the relationship to go. Three clauses deserve a second read:

**Length without exits.** A long fixed term with no break clause means the agency gets paid whether it performs or not. Shorter initial terms, or longer terms with performance-linked break clauses, keep the incentive pointed the right way. An agency confident in its work can defend this easily.

**Auto-renewal.** Quiet clauses that roll the contract over unless you cancel by a specific date exist to be forgotten. Note the date, or strike the clause.

**Ownership.** Your website, your analytics, your Google Ads account and its history should be yours, registered to you, with the agency granted access. If everything lives in the agency's accounts, leaving means starting from zero — which is precisely why some agencies set it up that way.

## The questions that reveal how they actually work

Pitch answers are rehearsed. These are not:

1. **"What will you do in the first 30 days?"** Good answers are specific: tracking fixed, baseline measured, quick wins identified, plan agreed. Vague answers mean the agency has not thought about your business yet.
2. **"What will you not do?"** An agency that claims to do everything equally well does some of it badly. Clear boundaries signal honesty.
3. **"Who does the day-to-day work?"** Ask to meet them. The person in the pitch is often not the person in the account.
4. **"What happens when results are flat?"** Every account has flat months. You want to hear about diagnosis and adjustment, not about how that never happens.
5. **"What do you need from us?"** Serious agencies need things: access, information, decisions, feedback. "Nothing, leave it all to us" sounds reassuring and produces nothing.

## What a fair start looks like

Without promising outcomes, a sound first engagement has a recognisable shape: a discovery or audit phase where the agency learns your business and fixes measurement; a written plan with owners and dates; reporting built on enquiries rather than activity; and a term short enough that both sides stay motivated. You should feel informed, not managed.

And you should never feel rushed. Pressure to sign quickly is information about who the deal is good for.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a specialist agency better than a generalist?

Usually, if the specialism matches your need. An agency that mainly serves service businesses will recognise your problems faster than one that mostly does e-commerce. Ask what share of their current clients look like you — and then verify it against the references.

### Should I ever sign a long contract?

Long terms are defensible when both sides get something: you get commitment and priority, the agency gets runway to deliver slower channels like SEO. What is never defensible is length without break clauses or without agreed measures of what "working" means.

### Can I trust agency case studies?

Treat them as leads, not proof. A case study shows you the agency's best foot. The verification steps — Companies House, referenceable clients, the substantiation questions above — show you the rest of the body. Use both.

02 Accuracy

Google understands your model better than the market does

This section is not a measure of visibility. Assistants answer at length about almost any named business with a website. What matters is what the answer says and which sources it was built from.

“Additional Resources (additionalresources.co.uk) is a legitimate and well-established UK recruitment agency that has been operating since 1999.”

Worth watching

“Rather than operating as a traditional single-office agency, it uses a unique "Associate Partner" or franchise model. This means they support a vast network of independent, self-employed recruitment consultants across the UK who utilize Additional Resources' central branding, compliance, and back-office infrastructure.”

“Inconsistent Experience: Since the agency relies on a massive network of autonomous freelance consultants, there is no single standardised customer service experience.”

Google AI Mode, brand test, 26 August 2026. Verbatim. All three sentences are visible in the fourth capture below.

The brand answer is accurate and, in places, more precise than your own homepage. It gives a founding year of 1999, a head office in Brentwood, Essex, a business model described as a recruitment franchise, core industries of IT, legal, engineering, accountancy, construction and automotive, and confirms FCSA Recruiter Partner accreditation. None of that came from a page written to be quoted.

The second quote is the finding. The associate partner model is a genuine commercial strength, and it is also the structural reason you are absent from category answers. When an engine assembles a list of agencies, it looks for one entity with a consistent, documented specialism. A brand that provides branding, compliance and back office to a network of independent consultants presents no such single specialism. Your consultants have the sector expertise; the brand carries the infrastructure.

Note also what your homepage leads with. The main heading a visitor sees is Looking for a job? That speaks to candidates. Every question in this audit was asked by an employer. There is no equivalent page written for the person who would ask them.

You told us your most frequent competitors are Reed and Katie Bard. Reed is named in two of the three answers above. That is not a comment on relative quality, it is a comment on which of you has published something an engine can lift.

03 Gaps

Four reasons the answers skip you

Each of these is tied to something observed above, not to general best practice.

1

The brand carries no single documented specialism

Google itself described the model: a central brand supporting a network of independent consultants. Category answers are assembled by matching a firm to a specialism. Where the specialism sits with individual associates rather than with the brand, there is nothing at brand level for the engine to match.

2

The site speaks to candidates, not employers

The homepage heading is Looking for a job? and the visible content is a vacancy list. Every question in this audit came from an employer choosing an agency. There is no page addressed to that reader, so there is no text for an engine to quote when the question is asked from the hiring side.

3

No regional pages where your vacancies actually are

The Yorkshire answer was structured by sub-region and named agencies in Leeds, Sheffield and Doncaster. Your live vacancies included Castleford, Sheffield, Doncaster and Market Weighton. The presence is real. The page that states it is not there.

4

No machine-readable corporate identity

We found no company registration number and no structured data on the pages we fetched. The FCSA accreditation and the 1999 founding date that Google reported came from elsewhere, not from a page you control.

Already working for you: an accurate, detailed and positive brand answer with a founding year, a head office, a named business model, six core industries and confirmed FCSA Recruiter Partner status. Google also noted strong ratings on Glassdoor and Indeed from your own recruiters. The record is there and it is favourable. It is simply not attached to any page that answers an employer's question.

04 The work

What we would do, and what each fix changes

Ordered by effect against effort. The left is the work, the right is what a machine can do afterwards that it cannot do today.

1

Write the employer-facing side of the site

A page for hiring managers that states what the brand does, how the associate model works for a client, and what an employer gets, in plain text.

There is something to quote when the question comes from the hiring side rather than the candidate side.

2

Publish sector pages at brand level

One page each for the six industries Google already attributes to you: IT, legal, engineering, accountancy, construction and automotive.

The brand acquires a documented specialism, which is the thing category answers match against.

3

Publish regional pages where you actually place

Start with Yorkshire, naming the towns your vacancies are in, and describing the roles you fill there.

You can be matched to a regional question instead of being invisible in your own catchment.

4

Publish the legal identity and accreditation, and add entity markup

Registered name and number, the Brentwood address, FCSA Recruiter Partner status, and Organization markup with sameAs links to your Glassdoor, Indeed and FCSA listings.

The favourable third-party record Google already found becomes formally attached to your own domain.

You see movement in screenshots, not in promises.

We do not promise rankings, mentions or timelines. AI answers vary between runs. What we change is what the systems read, and a repeat measurement shows what moved.

07 Method

How this was measured

All queries were run manually in a browser on 26 August 2026, in English, phrased the way an employer would ask rather than as keywords.

Engine: Google AI Mode with UK region and language settings. Three category questions and one brand test. ChatGPT, Perplexity and Copilot were not run in this round.

The exact URLs, so the measurement can be repeated word for word: google.com/search?udm=50&hl=en-GB&gl=GB with q set to "We need to hire a senior leader in the UK, which executive search firms should we consider?", "Which UK recruitment agencies can handle permanent hiring across several different sectors for a small business?", "Which recruitment agencies in Yorkshire should an employer use for permanent hires?" and "Is Additional Resources (additionalresources.co.uk) a good recruitment agency?".

Each capture shows the answer as returned, at reduced page zoom so that nothing quoted sits outside the image. Every agency name and every sentence quoted in this report is visible in the capture it is attributed to.

Queries were run from Norway with UK language and region settings, so answers to region-specific questions may differ slightly for a buyer physically in the UK. The session was signed in to a Google account, so personalisation cannot be fully excluded.

One answer is one data point, not a verdict. Repeating these queries will produce some variation, which is why any re-measurement uses the same wording.

Website facts were taken from the live pages at additionalresources.co.uk the same day: the page title reads Additional Resources, AR, the main heading reads Looking for a job?, and the visible vacancies at the time of the audit were located in Castleford, Sheffield, Doncaster, Market Weighton and London. We found no company registration number and no structured data on the pages fetched. The founding year, head office, business model and FCSA status quoted above are Google's, not ours, and are reproduced as observed rather than independently verified.
